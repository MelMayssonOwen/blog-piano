What Are the Different Types of Chords Used on the Piano?

Published 2026-10-30

TL;DR: Piano chords are notes played together, and beginners usually start with triads, which are three-note chords. The main types to learn first are major, minor, diminished, and augmented, then later sevenths, extended chords, suspended chords, inversions, and slash chords.

What a chord is

A chord is simply notes played together. On piano that usually means pressing a few keys at once so they team up instead of acting like lone musical freelancers. Most beginner piano chords use three notes. Those three note chords are called triads. As the basic chord guide from flowkey puts it, chords are notes played together and in most piano situations they have at least three notes.

The main chord families beginners should know

The first chord types most learners meet are major, minor, diminished, and augmented. That is not because music teachers enjoy making life complicated. It is because these chords show up constantly and each one has a clear sound plus a clear shape under the hands.

  • Major chords feel stable, bright, and very common in songs.
  • Minor chords use a similar shape to major chords but they sound darker or more pensive.
  • Diminished chords sound tense and unstable which makes them useful for creating forward motion.
  • Augmented chords sound bright but unsettled so they often show up as color or transition chords.

These are the building blocks that help beginners hear how harmony works. A lot of piano learning is really just your ears and fingers becoming friends with these shapes.

Chords built from more than three notes

Many piano chords go beyond the basic triad by adding extra tones. Beginners usually meet these later often when reading chord symbols in song charts.

  • Seventh chords are triads with an added seventh. They are used widely in pop, jazz, blues, and classical harmony.
  • Extended chords add more color tones beyond the seventh. These tend to appear later once basic chord shapes feel less mysterious.
  • Suspended chords replace the third with another note which creates a suspended, unresolved sound.

A helpful way to think about these is that they are still chords just with extra seasoning. Same meal, more spice.

Inversions and slash chords

The same chord can be played in different orders. That changes how it feels under the hand and how smoothly you can move between chords but it does not change what chord it is.

A chord in its normal order is called root position. In an inversion one of the other notes moves to the bottom.

Simple worked example

Take a C major triad:

  • Root position: C, E, G
  • First inversion: E, G, C

Both spell the same C major chord. The notes are just rearranged. On the keyboard that rearrangement can make the next chord easier to reach which is why inversions are so useful for beginners who want less hand jumping and more smooth motion.

Slash chords work in a similar way on paper. The note after the slash tells you which note is in the bass. So if you see a chord like C/E that means a C major chord with E in the bass.

How to think about chord types as an adult beginner

For adult beginners, chord types are less about memorizing a giant wall of symbols and more about building useful habits. Start with recognition. Can you hear major and minor? Can you spot the basic hand shape quickly? Can you move from one chord to another without your hand acting like it forgot its job?

That is the real win.

Focus on three practical goals:

  • Recognition, so you can identify common chord symbols and sounds.
  • Hand shape, so your fingers learn the feel of triads, sevenths, and inversions.
  • Smooth transitions, so chord changes sound connected instead of clunky.

If your chord changes feel uneven, a steady pulse can help. A metronome is useful because it gives you a consistent beat to aim for while you move between chords. That keeps the rhythm honest, which is always a nice thing when your hands are still negotiating with each other.

Quick recap for beginners

Start with major and minor triads. Those are the core shapes. Then add diminished, augmented, sevenths, and inversions as you encounter them in songs. Once those become familiar chord symbols stop looking like random punctuation and start looking like useful instructions.
